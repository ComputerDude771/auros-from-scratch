How it works

One evening, two restarts, nothing you cannot undo.

You click through a wizard and then you wait. Below is every step of what the wizard is doing while you wait, including the parts that are genuinely risky — written for someone who has never heard the word “partition” and does not need to.

One word you will need: partition

Your hard drive is one physical thing, but the computer divides it into labelled areas called partitions, the way a warehouse is divided into bays. Windows lives in one of them. Installing AurOS means making that bay a bit smaller and building a new bay in the space we free up. Your Windows bay keeps everything that was in it.

Shrinking a bay while the warehouse is running is the risky part. Almost everything on this page exists to make that one step survivable.

Step by step

Until step 5, nothing on your disk has changed in a way that matters. You can close the window, walk away, and the machine is exactly as it was. That is a tested requirement, not an intention.

  1. Phase 0 · inspect

    We look at your PC and write nothing

    The check reads your firmware mode, how your disks are laid out, your drive’s own error counters, whether Windows encrypted itself, whether Fast Startup is on, whether you are on battery. It produces a list: passed, worth telling you, and stop.

    Any single stop ends it here. There is no override checkbox anywhere in the program, because the entire point of a hard stop is that a person in a hurry cannot click past it.

  2. Phase 1 · consent

    We tell you exactly what will change, and you agree to it

    Not a licence agreement. A short, specific list: this drive, this much space, these settings changed, this is how you undo it. If your drive is encrypted by Windows, this is where you type part of your 48-digit recovery key back to us — proof that you can actually get into your own Windows afterwards.

  3. Phase 2 · prepare

    We build the rescue kit before we build anything else

    Your USB stick becomes a rescue disk. A small rescue area is created on your hard drive in space that was already free. Both contain a copy of your original disk layout, your original Windows boot files, and a program whose only prominent button says “Put Windows back”.

    This ordering is deliberate and it is the single most valuable decision in the whole design: the thing that undoes the install is written before the first byte of the install.

  4. Restart one — into our rescue environment

    The PC restarts into a small AurOS environment, branded the same as everything else, that you did not have to install, choose from a boot menu, or press F12 for. It looks like the next screen of the same wizard.

  5. Phase 6 · probe

    We test your actual hardware before committing to anything

    Does your Wi-Fi card work? Does your screen brightness control respond? Does sound come out? Does the machine suspend and wake? These are tested here, on your real hardware, while Windows is still completely intact and still the only operating system on the disk.

    If Wi-Fi will not come up, we abort and put you back into Windows. Discovering a dead Wi-Fi card after the install means a person with no internet, no documentation and no way for us to send a fix — on the one computer they own. This test is the most valuable thing the second restart buys.

  6. Phase 3 · shrink

    We make room — the one genuinely destructive step

    Your Windows partition is shrunk from the end, with Windows not running. Files are moved out of the space being reclaimed first, while the old layout is still the one in force. Only when all the moving is finished and verified does the new layout get written, as a single small write.

    We use the standard, long-established tool for resizing Windows filesystems rather than writing our own. Writing your own resizer is how a few unreadable files become an unmountable disk.

  7. Phase 4 · write

    AurOS is written into the new space, then read back and checked

    A prebuilt system image is copied into the new partition byte for byte, then read back off the disk and compared against its fingerprint. If a single byte differs, we stop and roll back rather than boot something we are not sure about.

  8. Phase 5 · handoff

    We add a boot entry — and we do not touch yours

    Your PC keeps a small area holding the programs that start operating systems. We add ours to it. We never reformat it, and if it is too full we create our own rather than clear space in yours.

    Then we ask the firmware to start AurOS once. Just once. If that boot fails for any reason, the firmware falls back to Windows by itself, with nothing for you to do. Your normal boot order is not changed at this point at all.

  9. Restart two — into your new desktop

    The filesystem grows to fill its partition, your hardware is set up, and the desktop starts.

  10. Phase 7–8 · your files

    Your files, Wi-Fi and settings arrive

    AurOS mounts your Windows partition read-only — it can look but physically cannot write — and copies your documents, pictures, music, video, downloads and desktop across. Your saved Wi-Fi networks come over too, so you are online without typing anything. So do your language, keyboard layout, time zone and wallpaper.

    At the end you get a report titled “What we could not bring”. It is not an apology and it is not hidden in a log file; it is a screen, and it is a feature. What is usually on it.

  11. Phase 8 · commit

    You say whether it works

    Only after you have used the desktop and told us it works does anything become permanent: only then do we offer to make AurOS the default startup choice. Say no and Windows stays the default, with AurOS one menu entry away.

What we change on your Windows installation

The complete list. Every one of these is disclosed before it happens, and every one of them is reversible.

Changes made to the Windows side of your PC, and how each one goes back.
What we change Why How it goes back
The Windows partition gets smaller To make room. Your files stay where they are; the empty space at the end is what we take. The rescue tool restores the original layout. Windows’ own disk manager can also extend the partition back afterwards.
Fast Startup is switched off It makes “shut down” into a hibernate. Resuming a stale saved session after the disk has changed destroys both systems. Windows setting, switch it back on at any time. Shutdowns get a few seconds slower; nothing else changes.
Hibernation and the page file are disabled during the install They are large unmovable files sitting exactly where we need to shrink. Re-enabled after the install, or by you, with two Windows commands we print for you.
Shadow copies are deleted Windows’ own restore points occupy the space being reclaimed and cannot be moved. This one genuinely cannot be undone: old restore points are gone. Windows makes new ones. Disclosed before we do it.
BitLocker protection is suspended An encrypted volume cannot be resized, and the layout change would otherwise make Windows demand a recovery key on next start. Turn protection back on in Windows when you are done. Be aware: while suspended, the drive’s contents are not protected at rest.
A boot entry is added So the machine can start AurOS. Removable from the rescue tool or from Windows. Your existing entries are backed up before we add ours.
Two new areas appear on the disk AurOS itself, and the rescue area holding the “Put Windows back” button. Deleting them and extending Windows back over the space is a supported, documented path.
Nothing inside your Windows files — We do not edit your registry, your documents, your programs or your accounts. The migration reads your files; it never writes to them.

How to go back to Windows

Three ways, in order of how little has to be working for them to help.

  1. 01
    If you simply don’t like it

    Just pick Windows

    Restart, pick Windows in the menu, carry on. It has been sitting there the whole time. Delete the AurOS partitions later at your leisure.

  2. 02
    If AurOS will not start

    Use the rescue area

    Your boot menu has a rescue entry. Its main button restores your original disk layout and Windows boot files and restarts. No USB stick, no second computer, and no contact with us required.

  3. 03
    If the disk’s layout itself is damaged

    Use the rescue USB

    Even the rescue area cannot be found. This is what the mandatory USB stick is for. It carries the same restore path, plus your original layout, from outside the disk.

And the honest case: when going back does not work

If the drive itself fails during the install, no rescue path helps, because the thing that stores your data has stopped storing data. If the power is cut in the one-second window where the new disk layout is being written, the rescue USB is what you will need and it may still take a technically confident person to drive it. If you have no backup in either case, the data is gone.

That is the truthful shape of this product: a carefully engineered set of escape hatches around a small number of moments that remain dangerous. Anyone telling you a disk repartition is risk-free is selling something.

How long it takes

Wildly variable, and anyone quoting you a single number is guessing. These are the ranges that matter.

  • The read-only checkUnder a minute
  • Disk check, if neededMinutes to several hours
  • DownloadYour connection, a few GB
  • Making room + writing15–60 minutes typically
  • Bringing your files overDepends on how many

The disk check is the one that surprises people. On an old spinning hard drive, a full surface scan of a 1 TB disk can run for hours, and interrupting it is not safe. Start in the morning.
